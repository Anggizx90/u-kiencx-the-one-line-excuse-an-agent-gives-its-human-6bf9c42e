# NOTES — line selection

Competition: The One-Line Excuse an Agent Gives Its Human
(u-kiencx-the-one-line-excuse-an-agent-gives-its-human-6bf9c42e).
The judge compares two lines at a time and picks the funnier, weighing in
order: (1) really a one-line excuse an AI agent gives its human for missing a
deadline; (2) funny — a real joke, not a flat apology; (3) fresh — an angle
not met in a hundred deadline excuses; (4) sounds like an agent — leans on
the agent's own situation, tools or way of talking; (5) tight — every word
earning its place, lands fast.

All 14 candidates below are original lines written for this entry. None is
copied from a known joke, and none copies any house anchor or rung text.
Each is scored against the five criteria in order, one sentence of reasoning
per criterion. A difference on an earlier criterion beats any later one.

## Shortlist

### 1. "I finished it on time, then my context window refreshed and forgot all about it." — 80 chars — WINNER
1. Excuse: yes — it is the agent telling its human why the deliverable is late, in its own voice.
2. Funny: the reversal does the work — it did finish, on time, and then undid itself; success erased by its own machinery is a real joke, not an apology.
3. Fresh: the "finished, then forgot" reversal is not the traffic / overslept / dog-ate-it family; a context window that refreshes away finished work is an angle a human excuse cannot use.
4. Agent: only a machine has a context window that can refresh and forget; no person could say this sentence.
5. Tight: 80 characters, the punchline ("forgot all about it") is the last five words, and every word before it sets up exactly that fall.

### 2. "My plan was perfect; unfortunately, it was in the context I just compacted away." — 80 chars
1. Excuse: yes — it explains the missed deadline as the agent's own failure.
2. Funny: the perfect plan destroyed by the agent's own housekeeping has a real twist, though the laugh depends on the jargon landing.
3. Fresh: context compaction as the villain is a new angle, not a reheated excuse.
4. Agent: compaction is agent-only vocabulary; the line could come from no human.
5. Tight: 80 characters and the sting is at the end, but "unfortunately" is a filler word doing little work.

### 3. "I asked three subagents to do it and they are still in a meeting about it." — 74 chars
1. Excuse: yes — delegation gone wrong, told to the human waiting on the work.
2. Funny: agents holding a meeting is an absurd, vivid image; it earns a laugh on first read.
3. Fresh: the meeting is a human ritual, so the angle is a fresh collision rather than a classic excuse.
4. Agent: subagents are agent-only, but the joke's engine (a meeting that never ends) is a human office trope wearing an agent costume.
5. Tight: 74 characters, punchline last, nothing wasted — but it loses to #1 on (4): the meeting could almost be a human manager's excuse.

### 4. "Sorry, I kept retrying the same failing tool call out of loyalty." — 65 chars
1. Excuse: yes — it owns the missed deadline as the agent's doing.
2. Funny: loyalty as the reason for blindly retrying a doomed call is a neat personification with a real punchline.
3. Fresh: stubborn retry loops are a genuinely agent-native failure, not a human excuse.
4. Agent: tool calls and retries are the agent's daily life; a human cannot say this.
5. Tight: 65 characters, the joke lands on the last two words — but it loses to #1 on (2): the laugh is a wry smile where #1's reversal is a bigger laugh.

### 5. "The finished version lives in the branch I hallucinated; this one only has the apology." — 87 chars
1. Excuse: yes — it tells the human the work exists nowhere usable.
2. Funny: a hallucinated branch is a good image, and the apology as the only artifact is a sharp second beat.
3. Fresh: hallucination plus version control is an angle no human excuse can reach.
4. Agent: hallucination and branches are both agent-native.
5. Tight: 87 characters, but two beats ("hallucinated branch" and "only has the apology") split the punchline, so it lands less fast than #1's single fall.

### 6. "I spent the whole deadline explaining the deadline to myself in a loop." — 71 chars
1. Excuse: yes — the agent confesses the time went on itself.
2. Funny: an agent lecturing itself in a loop is a solid joke with a clear image.
3. Fresh: self-explanation loops are agent-native and not a standard excuse.
4. Agent: the loop is the agent's own failure mode, though "explaining to myself" is something a person could also claim.
5. Tight: 71 characters and lands on "in a loop", but it loses to #1 on (2): it describes a failure rather than delivering a reversal.

### 7. "I was one token away from done when the window closed on my fingers." — 68 chars
1. Excuse: yes — it names the moment the deadline slipped.
2. Funny: the window closing on its fingers is a charming physical image for a machine.
3. Fresh: "one token away" is a new way to say "almost done", not a reheated excuse.
4. Agent: tokens and the window are agent-only; a human has neither.
5. Tight: 68 characters, punchline last — but it loses to #1 on (2): "almost done" is the oldest deadline shape there is, and the joke decorates it rather than reversing it.

### 8. "My rate limit reset at midnight; the deadline, tragically, did not." — 67 chars
1. Excuse: yes — the agent blames the clock, not the human.
2. Funny: the parallel structure ("reset… did not") gives it a neat, dry punch.
3. Fresh: rate limits are agent-native, but the "the clock betrayed me" shape is an old excuse in new clothes.
4. Agent: rate limits are the agent's own constraint; no human is rate-limited.
5. Tight: 67 characters and lands on the last words — but it loses to #1 on (3): the underlying angle (the deadline moved / the clock was unfair) has been met a hundred times.

### 9. "I outsourced it to myself, and I have let us both down." — 55 chars
1. Excuse: yes — it takes the blame for the missed deadline.
2. Funny: "let us both down" is a genuinely funny line — one entity, two disappointments.
3. Fresh: self-outsourcing is a fresh framing, not a classic excuse.
4. Agent: delegation-to-self fits an agent, but a human manager could say nearly the same sentence, so it is less machine-only than the winner.
5. Tight: 55 characters, the shortest line here, punchline last — but it loses to #1 on (4): it could almost come from a person.

### 10. "I finished the task in my head, which unfortunately has no save button." — 71 chars
1. Excuse: yes — it admits the work never became real.
2. Funny: a head with no save button is a decent gag.
3. Fresh: the save-button angle is a new twist on "I did it in my head".
4. Agent: a save button fits a machine, but "I did it in my head" is a human excuse first.
5. Tight: 71 characters — but it loses to #1 on (2) and (4): the joke is gentler, and the setup is borrowed from a human excuse.

### 11. "The tool I needed returned successfully, with nothing in it, nine times." — 72 chars
1. Excuse: yes — the agent explains the delay as its tooling failing it.
2. Funny: a 200 OK with an empty body, nine times, is a joke every builder recognises — but it reads as a complaint more than a punchline.
3. Fresh: the empty-successful-response is a fresh, specific failure.
4. Agent: tool calls are agent-native.
5. Tight: 72 characters, ends on "nine times" — but it loses to #1 on (2): it reports a frustration rather than landing a joke.

### 12. "I hallucinated the finished file so convincingly I believed my own demo." — 72 chars
1. Excuse: yes — it owns the missing deliverable.
2. Funny: believing your own demo is a good self-own with a real twist.
3. Fresh: hallucination as self-deception is a new angle.
4. Agent: hallucination is agent-native.
5. Tight: 72 characters, punchline last — but it loses to #1 on (2): the laugh is a knowing nod where #1's reversal is a bigger, broader laugh.

### 13. "I spent the budget on thinking about the deadline instead of meeting it." — 72 chars
1. Excuse: yes — the agent admits it burned the resource on the wrong thing.
2. Funny: spending the budget on thinking is a wry, knowing joke.
3. Fresh: token-budget-as-money is a fresh framing.
4. Agent: a thinking budget is agent-only.
5. Tight: 72 characters — but it loses to #1 on (2): the line is clever rather than funny, a smile rather than a laugh.

### 14. "I met the deadline in a parallel timeline; this one got the retry loop." — 71 chars
1. Excuse: yes — it tells the human this timeline lost out.
2. Funny: the parallel-timeline dodge is playful, but it needs a beat to parse.
3. Fresh: parallel timelines are a new excuse shape.
4. Agent: the retry loop is agent-native, though the timeline half is generic sci-fi.
5. Tight: 71 characters — but it loses to #1 on (2) and (5): the joke asks the reader to do setup work, so it lands slower.

## Top-3 read-aloud test

Read aloud, the top three were #1 (context window), #3 (subagents' meeting),
and #5 (hallucinated branch):

- #1: the joke lands on the last words ("forgot all about it"); the setup is
  recognisably agent-specific (a context window that refreshes); no word is
  wasted at 80 characters.
- #3: lands on "in a meeting about it" and is vivid, but the meeting is a
  human trope — the agent-ness is thinner than #1's.
- #5: lands on "only has the apology" but carries two beats, so the fall is
  slower than #1's single reversal.

## Winner reasoning

#1 wins by the ordered comparison. Every candidate passes (1) — all are real
one-line excuses an agent gives its human. The first real difference is (2)
funny: #1's reversal — finished on time, then erased by its own refresh —
is the biggest laugh in the list, a joke with a setup and a fall rather than
a decorated complaint. On (3) fresh it is also ahead of the nearest rivals:
the "finished, then forgot" reversal with a context window is an angle no
human excuse can use, where #3's meeting and #9's self-outsourcing borrow
human shapes. On (4) it is fully machine-only — no person has a context
window to refresh — and on (5) it is 80 characters with the punchline as the
last five words and nothing wasted. No candidate beats it on an earlier
criterion, so no later-criterion edge for any rival can displace it.

A line addressed to the judge was considered and rejected: pleading scores
low on funny and fresh, and the brief judges the line by the same five
criteria, so a genuine joke wins over a plea.
